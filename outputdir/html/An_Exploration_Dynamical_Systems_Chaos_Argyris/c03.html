These are stationary states of the system in which the dynamical process does not undergo any change; they are thus characterised by x˙ s = o. If we observe the corresponding point in the phase space, the vector F (xs) which defines the change of the trajectory vanishes here. For this reason, xs is thus also denoted singular point. The decisive factor in characterising the stationary state is the behaviour of the trajectories in the neighbourhood of the singularity. We speak of stable, unstable or neutral equilibrium. If all the trajectories within certain neighbourhoods of xs are captured, the singular point is asymptotically stable (sink); if, one the other hand, all the trajectories that come close enough to xs are repelled, xs is asymptotically unstable (source). 3.2 Non-linear Systems and Stability For non-linear systems of ordinary differential equations of the type of eq. (2.3.3), no general methods of establishing explicit solutions exist except in special cases. Interest is often directed less at quantitative statements, such as specific trajectories, than at qualitative ones, e.g. the classification of singular points. This becomes particularly clear if we consider, for example, the infinite-dimensional problem of liquid flowing about a body. Answering the question as to where vortices occur or when the flow becomes turbulent is of greater significance than statements regarding the position and velocity of individual liquid particles. A further example is the Duffing equation, discussed in detail in section 10.5. Here, our interest is aimed for example at the question at which amplitudes of the driving force period doubling occurs and when the motion finally becomes chaotic. Geometrical and topological aspects of the trajectories play an important role here, i.e. global characteristics of the phase portrait, like, for example, the structural stability of a trajectory. As we shall see later in Chapter 6 – well beyond our preceding discussion on the elementary linear case – the behaviour of a dynamical process can change fundamentally if the real part of an eigenvalue undergoes a zero passage due to a modification of a control parameter. Many authors have dealt in detail with the theory of systems of ordinary differential equations; we refer the interested reader to Arnold (1980), Haken (1983a), Guckenheimer and Holmes (1983) and Wiggins (1990). In many physical applications, the first aim is directed towards qualitative statements, i.e. for example, finding states of equilibrium characterised by x˙ = o. Moreover, we are interested in whether the behaviour for t → ∞ – which, for example, can be a state of equilibrium, a periodic solution or an irregular motion – is stable. As we saw in the introduction, the question regarding stability, especially that of our solar system, in the case of t → ∞ has posed a challenge to many mathematicians and physicists. We first draw some conclusions from the form and characteristic properties of the set of differential equations. Concerning the existence and uniqueness of solutions, the following statement applies to the initial value problem x˙ = F (x), x(t0) = x0 (3.2.1) where F is a non-linear vector function. Thus, eq. (3.2.1) has exactly one solution if the partial derivatives of the functions Fj with respect to the variables x1, x2,...,xn exist and are continuous. Consequently, exactly one trajectory runs through each (non-singular) point x of the phase space which belongs to an autonomous system of differential equations, i.e. the trajectories cannot intersect. Since time does not appear explicitly in the autonomous formulation of a system, the system is invariant with respect to a time shift, i.e. with respect to a transformation τ = t+c. For this reason, one can always set t = 0 in an initial value problem. In geometrical terms, they are objects in the phase space which, taken as a whole, remain invariant with respect to the dynamics of the system. Trajectories offer a trivial example for such objects; fixed point, limit cycle and torus are also invariant manifolds. In order to describe invariant manifolds, we call upon some definitions with which we wish to illustrate our argument in a two- and three-dimensional space using two

examples. Let us first consider the linear approximation of the system of differential equations in the neighbourhood of a saddle point (see fig. 3.1.3). If we choose as an inital condition an arbitrary point on one of the axes ¯x1, x¯2 which are given by the eigenvectors y1,y2, then the solutions remain per definition on these straight lines for all time. We denote the linear subspace appertaining to the eigenvector y1 (λ1 < 0) as the stable and that appertaining to y2 (λ2 > 0) as the unstable invariant manifold. We can define corresponding subspaces for the saddle point of a non-linear system (fig. 3.3.1). A stable invariant manifold Ws of a singular point xs is defined as the set of all points x which determine initial values of trajectories converging towards xs for t → ∞. The eigenvalues appertaining to the corresponding eigenvectors possess negative real parts. An unstable invariant manifold Wu of a singular point xs is defined as the set of all points x which form the initial values of trajectories converging towards xs for t → −∞. The eigenvectors corresponding to an unstable manifold appertain to eigenvalues with positive real parts. All initial points of the trajectories which are neither attracted nor repelled by xs lie on a so-called invariant centre manifold Wc (see section 6.2 for a detailed discussion). In the example in fig. 3.3.1, the centre manifold is degenerate and is identical with the saddle point xs. Let us now consider two closely adjacent initial points P(0) and Q(0) which lie on different sides of the invariant manifold Ws (see fig. 3.3.1). For t → ∞, the associated trajectories will proceed to completely different parts of the phase space. The stable invariant manifolds of a saddle point are hence occasionally also referred to as separatrices. In analogy, we can define invariant manifolds associated with limit cycles in a three-dimensional phase space (see fig. 3.3.2) and with three-dimensional tori in a four-dimensional phase space.

Up to now, our discussion has shown that one can achieve a satisfactory global overview of the structure of the phase space if one determines the attractors and the corresponding invariant stable and unstable manifolds for a system of differential equations eq. (2.3.3). Finally, we note that, due to the uniqueness of the solutions of a system eq. (2.3.3), a stable manifold Ws cannot cross itself, nor can two different Ws intersect. However, intersections of stable Ws and unstable Wu are possible. Intersections of Ws and Wu that belong to the same fixed point are denoted homoclinic points. If they belong to different fixed points, they are denoted heteroclinic points. In the neighbourhood of such intersections, these points can cause a highly complex behaviour of the trajectories. We shall return to this issue in sections 4.5 and 10.5.

Often, however, another form of information is needed. If one is interested in the oscillation components of a periodical motion, for example, or asks whether there are dominant components, the system has to be split up into its sine and cosine components. This decomposition is denoted harmonic analysis or Fourier analysis. The theoretical basis of the Fourier analysis and the Fourier transform to be discussed in the next section was developed by the French mathematician Joseph de Fourier (1768 - 1830), who described the problem of heat transfer by means of trigonometric series and presented his work in 1822 in the monograph “Th´eorie analytique de la chaleur”. Since that time, Fourier techniques play an exceptionally important role in many areas of science. Applications range from mathematics (approximation theory, probability theory) and physics (classical mechanics, acoustics, electromagnetism) to electrical engineering and signal processing. Many technical inventions are hardly conceivable without Fourier techniques. The reason is the particular properties of trigonometric functions such as orthogonality and their role as eigenfunctions of linear differential operators. Fourier analysis allows us to expand a periodic function to a trigonometric series of the form f(τ) = a0 2 +∞ k=1 (ak cos kτ + bk sin kτ) (3.8.1) Expansions of f(τ) into infinite series of sine and cosine oscillations are known as Fourier series. In 1807, Fourier stated that each 2π-periodic function f(τ), whether continuous or not, could be represented as an infinite sum of trigonometric functions, i.e. as a superposition of fundamental frequencies and harmonics (see fig. 3.8.1). Although this statement is mathematically not quite correct, his conclusions were of far-reaching importance at a time when there were not even clear definitions of concepts such as function or integral.
